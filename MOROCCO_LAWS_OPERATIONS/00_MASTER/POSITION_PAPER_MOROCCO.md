## POSITION PAPER — KINGDOM OF MOROCCO

**Delegation:** Kingdom of Morocco
**Committee:** United Nations General Assembly
**Agenda:** Evaluating the need for a binding international convention on the regulation of Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS)

### I. Introduction and National Perspective
The Kingdom of Morocco approaches the question of lethal autonomous weapons systems not as a spectator but as a State party to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), a member of the African Group and the Non-Aligned Movement, an endorser of international frameworks for the responsible military use of artificial intelligence, and a nation that operates — and is building the capacity to produce — modern drone technology under strict human command.

Morocco's conclusion is direct: **the need for a legally binding international convention is established.** It was established by the General Assembly itself, which adopted resolutions 78/241 (2023), 79/62 (2024) and 80/57 (2025) with overwhelming majorities — the last by 164 votes, including Morocco's. It was established by the Secretary-General and the International Committee of the Red Cross, who have jointly called for negotiations to conclude a binding instrument. And it was established by the African Group, which declared at the CCW Group of Governmental Experts in March 2026 that African States are "ready and committed" to moving toward negotiations on the basis of the GGE's rolling text.

The question before this committee is therefore not *whether* the world needs law on autonomous weapons. It is *how, where, and by when* that law will be negotiated.

### II. The Two-Tier Approach — Morocco's Position on Ban, Regulate, or Permit
Morocco rejects the false trichotomy of "ban everything, regulate everything, or permit everything." The law has a better tool: **prohibition where humanity must draw a line, regulation where technology can serve lawful ends.** Morocco supports a two-tier instrument that (1) **prohibits** weapons systems that select and engage targets without meaningful human control, or that cannot be used in compliance with international humanitarian law, and (2) **regulates** all other autonomous systems through binding standards on design, testing, deployment, transparency and use. This mirrors the architecture of the CCW itself, the framework that banned blinding lasers before they were entrenched, and reflects the recommendation of the International Committee of the Red Cross.

### III. Defining Meaningful Human Control
Morocco holds that meaningful human control is an operational standard, not a slogan. Its elements are already agreed in the GGE rolling text: context-appropriate human judgment over targeting; predictability of system behavior; explainability of decisions; the ability of a human operator to intervene and terminate; and accountability through the chain of command. Morocco calls for the negotiation process to codify this standard, informed by existing national practices — including Morocco's own, under which every use of force is authorized and commanded by a person.

### IV. Accountability and Civilian Protection
Responsibility for the use of force can never be transferred to machines. Morocco supports binding national obligations: legal review of weapons systems under Article 36 of Additional Protocol I, testing and evaluation before deployment, audit trails preserving individual accountability, and training of operators. Under international humanitarian law, the principles of distinction, proportionality and precaution must govern any autonomous system, and any weapon that cannot comply must not be used. The protection of civilians — and of the right to life — is the purpose of this convention.

### V. Transparency, Export Controls, and the Role of the United Nations
Morocco supports transparency and confidence-building among States — including exchanges of national policies and legal-review practice — and effective national export and transfer controls, with end-use safeguards, to prevent acquisition by non-State actors. Morocco entrusts the United Nations with the role it has played in every arms-control regime: convening, standard-setting, reporting and review. In particular, Morocco calls on the Seventh Review Conference of the CCW (16–20 November 2026) to adopt a mandate to negotiate, and should that process stall, the General Assembly retains the authority to convene an inclusive diplomatic conference.

### VI. Protecting Developing Nations and Civilian AI
Autonomy is proliferating downward — to terrorists and criminals equipped with off-the-shelf drones. Developing States must not be left defenseless, either militarily or legally. Morocco therefore insists that the convention include a technical assistance and capacity-building programme — coordinated with the African Union, building on the Continental Artificial Intelligence Strategy — funded by voluntary contributions and open to all States. At the same time, Morocco affirms that nothing in the convention shall restrict civilian AI innovation, research, or the use of artificial intelligence for intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance, logistics and cybersecurity functions that do not involve autonomous targeting without human control. Morocco's own national AI strategy — a $10 billion, 50,000-job programme launched in January 2026 — depends on that distinction, and so does the world's.

### VII. Conclusion
Morocco regulates what it operates. We ask every delegation that operates technology to do the same. The elements are ready. The majority is ready. What is missing is a calendar — and this committee can provide it. Morocco calls for a negotiation mandate this November, a defined timeline, and a convention that Africa helps write, not one that is written for Africa. A calendar, not more analysis.
