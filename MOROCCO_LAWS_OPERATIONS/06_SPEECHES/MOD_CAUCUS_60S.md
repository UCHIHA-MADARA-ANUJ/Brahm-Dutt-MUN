# 🎤 MODERATED CAUCUS SPEECH BANK — 60 SECONDS (CONFIRMED TIME LIMIT)
**~150–155 words each ≈ 60 seconds at speaking pace. Practice aloud. Adapt the last line to the actual subtopic voted.**

---

## 1. SUBTOPIC: "The negotiation mandate and timeline"
"Mr./Madam Chair — the Kingdom of Morocco asks this committee one question: what exactly are we still discussing? Ten years of negotiations produced eleven guiding principles. The Group of Governmental Experts produced a rolling text with the elements of an instrument — characterization, human control, prohibitions, accountability. The Secretary-General and the ICRC called for a legally binding instrument. In December, 164 states — including Morocco — voted for it. The African Group, speaking for our continent's States parties to the CCW, declared in March that we are ready and committed to moving toward negotiations on the basis of that text. The need is proven. The elements are mature. What is missing is a calendar. Morocco proposes this committee call on the CCW's Seventh Review Conference in November to launch negotiations — and if Geneva cannot decide, this Assembly convenes the conference itself in 2027. A calendar, not more analysis. I yield."

## 2. SUBTOPIC: "The two-tier approach: prohibitions and regulations"
"Mr./Madam Chair — the debate before us is often framed as a choice between banning everything or permitting everything. That is a false choice, and Morocco rejects it. The two-tier approach is not a compromise between camps; it is a distinction law has always made. Prohibit what cannot be lawful — systems that select and engage targets without meaningful human control, systems that cannot comply with international humanitarian law. Regulate what can be lawful — predictable systems under human control, subject to binding standards on design, testing, deployment and use. This is how the CCW, the Chemical Weapons Convention and the Ottawa Convention were built: prohibitions where humanity draws a line, regulation where technology serves responsible ends. Morocco operates modern drone systems, and precisely for that reason we demand the line be drawn at human control. Prohibit what cannot be lawful; regulate what can be. I yield."

## 3. SUBTOPIC: "Defining meaningful human control"
"Mr./Madam Chair — 'meaningful human control' is not a slogan; it is an operational standard, and it is already being practiced. Its elements are in the GGE's rolling text: context-appropriate human judgment over targeting; predictability of system behavior; explainability of decisions; the ability of a human operator to intervene and terminate operations; and accountability that follows the chain of command. Morocco applies this standard in the field today — a person decides, a person authorizes, a person remains accountable. The delegations that say this cannot be defined should explain how their own militaries practice it daily. What cannot be defined cannot be practiced — and yet it is. The task of this committee is not to invent the standard. It is to codify it. We therefore support defining meaningful human control within the negotiation process, informed by national practices, and we offer Morocco's own experience as a contribution. I yield."

## 4. SUBTOPIC: "Accountability and legal review"
"Mr./Madam Chair — when an autonomous system is involved in unlawful force, who answers? The machine cannot. The algorithm cannot. Responsibility must remain with humans — designers, commanders, operators — through the entire lifecycle. This is why Morocco calls for binding national obligations: legal review of weapons systems before deployment, including their autonomous functions; testing, evaluation, validation and verification; audit trails that preserve accountability; and training for operators. These are not theoretical demands — they are the disciplines the REAIM Pathways to Action declaration codified, which Morocco endorsed in February, and which responsible militaries already apply. A treaty without accountability is a memo. An accountability framework without a treaty is unenforceable. The two belong together, and this resolution puts them together. I yield."

## 5. SUBTOPIC: "Capacity building and the Global South"
"Mr./Madam Chair — let us be honest about who is in this room. The states that will negotiate this instrument include dozens of delegations with limited legal, technical and testing capacity — including many of my African brothers and sisters. If the convention is written only by those who already have the expertise, it will be implemented only by them — and it will fail universality. That is why capacity building is not a footnote; it is a condition of this treaty's legitimacy. Morocco proposes a dedicated technical assistance programme, coordinated by UNODA and UNIDIR with the African Union, funded through voluntary contributions, covering legal review training, policy drafting and testing expertise — and Morocco pledges South–South cooperation as its first contribution. Access with rules beats denial without rules. The Global South is not a spectator to this treaty; it is a co-author. I yield."

## 6. SUBTOPIC: "Non-state actors and proliferation"
"Mr./Madam Chair — there is a detail this debate too often overlooks: autonomy is proliferating downward. Off-the-shelf drones, commercial software, open-source targeting models — the same technology is accessible to insurgents, terrorists and criminals, and Morocco has raised this from direct experience. The threat is not only that a great power automates its arsenal; it is that a non-state actor buys a kill decision. The convention must therefore include transfer and export safeguards, end-use controls, and a clear obligation to prevent acquisition by non-state actors. Human control is not a luxury of large armies — it is the condition of lawful force, and it must be denied to no one and available to everyone. This is why Morocco supports a binding instrument, not voluntary guidelines: because proliferation does not wait for goodwill. I yield."

## 7. SUBTOPIC: "The role of the UN and monitoring"
"Mr./Madam Chair — the guide to our work asks what role the United Nations should play in monitoring compliance. Morocco's answer: the role it plays in every arms-control regime — convening, standard-setting, reporting and review. National implementation first: legal reviews, testing and reporting. Then international review: a conference of States parties, periodic review conferences, and reports of the Secretary-General. And if the existing process cannot deliver, this Assembly — with the authority it exercised for the landmine ban, the cluster-munitions convention and the Arms Trade Treaty — convenes an inclusive diplomatic conference. The UN does not need new powers; it needs a decision to use the powers it has. This committee is where that decision begins. I yield."

## 8. SUBTOPIC: "Civilian AI and dual-use technology"
"Mr./Madam Chair — let us be clear about what this convention is not. It is not a ban on artificial intelligence. It is not a ban on dual-use technology. It is not a barrier to civilian innovation. Morocco's own national AI strategy — built with partners like Mistral AI, with sovereign data centers and an Al-Jazari institute network — depends on an open, innovative AI ecosystem. What we are banning is a single, narrow, unacceptable function: machines deciding who lives and who dies without human control. Surveillance, logistics, cybersecurity, decision-support — all remain lawful, and this resolution says so explicitly. The convention protects the civilian economy while restraining the one application humanity cannot accept. That is not anti-technology. It is pro-humanity. I yield."

## DELIVERY RULES
- **Opening 3 seconds:** "Mr./Madam Chair, distinguished delegates —" (never "ladies and gentlemen").
- **One fact max per speech** (the number bank); the rest is position + proposal.
- **End with either an ask** ("I yield to questions") or a concrete proposal ("Morocco proposes...") — never trail off.
- **Eye contact with the chair during the first line; with the room from the second sentence.**
- **60s = 150-155 words.** If you speak fast, cut the second example sentence. If slow, extend with the guide-question tie-in: "This answers the guide's question on [X]."
- Keep 2 spare 60s variants in your head: one US-friendly (transparency/legal review), one maximalist-friendly (anti-personnel prohibition as negotiation priority) — for bloc-pleasing moments.
